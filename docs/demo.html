Why Your Brain Needs Boredom

Sample article for testing ReadAnki · 4 min read

Most of us can barely remember the last time we were truly bored. The moment a queue slows down or a train is delayed, we reach for our phones without even thinking about it. Yet a growing number of researchers argue that this habit comes at a cost we have only just begun to understand.

When the mind is left with nothing in particular to do, it does not simply switch off. Instead, it starts to wander, drifting between memories, plans and half-formed ideas. Scientists who study this state say it is far from a waste of time: it is when the brain sorts through what it has recently taken in and connects it to what it already knows.

A quiet kind of work

In one well-known type of experiment, volunteers are asked to do something deliberately dull, such as copying numbers from a phone book, before being given a creative task. Those who had been bored first tend to come up with more original answers than those who went straight into the challenge.

The explanation, researchers suggest, is that boredom pushes us to look for stimulation inside our own heads. Having run out of things to notice around them, people begin to play with ideas they would otherwise have dismissed.

"Boredom is not the enemy of creativity," one psychologist put it. "It is often the doorway to it."

What we lose by staying busy

The problem is that smartphones have made it almost impossible to be bored for more than a few seconds. Every gap in the day is filled with messages, videos or news, leaving the mind little room to wander. Over time, some experts worry, we may be trading deep thinking for a constant stream of small distractions.

This does not mean that technology is to blame for everything, nor that we should throw our devices away. But it does suggest that the occasional empty moment, far from being something to avoid, might be worth protecting.

Learning to do nothing

For those who want to get used to boredom again, the advice is surprisingly simple. Leave your phone in your bag on a short walk. Wait for the kettle to boil without checking your messages. Look out of the window on the bus instead of scrolling.

At first, it may feel uncomfortable, even a little anxious. But people who stick with it often report that ideas they had been struggling with for weeks suddenly fall into place. In the long run, giving your mind permission to do nothing may turn out to be one of the most productive things you can do.
